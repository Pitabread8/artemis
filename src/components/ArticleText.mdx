**Artemis II**, which safely returned on April 20, 2026, marked a historic return to **manned lunar spaceflight**, but it is also a historic launch because of the scale of industrial coordination required to make it possible.

The broader Artemis program carries an **unprecedented budget** measured in the tens of billions of dollars, and a large share of that cost comes from the fact that so many parts of the launch stack had to be custom designed, manufactured, integrated, and certified for this mission architecture in order for the SLS to be **"the only rocket that can send Orion, astronauts, and supplies to the Moon in a single mission."**

<InlinePriceDiagram />

As said, Artemis is an unprecedented mission in terms of its scope and scale. Of course, this means it couldn't have been done alone. For Artemis, NASA turned to its reliable team of **private contractors**. NASA's team is a highly specialized network that the organization has been in collaboration with since lunar spaceflight was first conceptualized in the 1960s.

Taking both of these charts together now, we can see for example that the most expensive section of the Artemis II SLS was the Core Stage, and that the Core Stage was designed and fabricated primarily by **Boeing**. Boeing has been at the heart of NASA's spaceflight enterprise, having a hand in basically all of its missions since the initial 20th century Space Race. Zooming in on this historic race, we can look at the Apollo 11's Saturn V rocket to see if any patterns emerge:

Here, even though the entire rocket was **nine times less** than Artemis II, we can see that Boeing (marked blue) still played an significantly larger role in putting a man on the moon the first time. Another interesting difference between the two generation's industrial landscape is the types of players involved. In 2026, all of NASA's corporate partners were multi-billion dollar engineering firms. In 1967 though, there were many smaller companies involved that have since **merged** into these megacorps, as indicated by the new shades of each color. Nonetheless, in continuing our exploration, looking at other past lunar missions reveals a similar breakdown; Boeing and its subsidiaries are again the most prominent.

The companies that worked on the rockets above are key players in NASA overall, as they're awarded the agency's **largest contracts**. Take a look at how their involvement has progressed over the past 18 years. You'll notice a wide variety of colors due to the fact that NASA employs a large number of subcontractors per mission. The work is widely distributed between companies with each company specializing in certain aspects of a spaceship. This method of NASA integrating components from various companies to form one spaceship is a model they will continue to use while other private companies like SpaceX have shifted to a vertically integrated model. This means that SpaceX develops the full end-to-end spaceship completely in-house.

As the bar graph timeline progresses, one company (highlighted in a distinct red) slowly makes its way up the ranks. This company is Space Exploration Technologies Corp., better known as **SpaceX**. While SpaceX did not work on the Artemis II mission specifically, it is impossible to discuss the modern space age without it. While companies like Boeing, Lockheed Martin, Northrop Gruman, and more have played a huge role in developing spaceships. SpaceX has redefined the market by shifting to a full in-house production of spaceships. They have achieved a full vertical integration of manufacturing spaceships, getting rid of the subcontractor model resulting in a huge increase in the number of spaceflights due to the speed and cost effectiveness with which they can manufacture new spaceships.

Shown above is the cost effectiveness of SpaceX with relation to manufacturing spaceships. Many of the components and processes that come with manufacturing spaceships have proven to be cheaper for SpaceX due to their vertical integration model. The recent Artemis II launch could be as much as **$5 Billion** cheaper if SpaceX were to manufacture it. While NASA pays a premium for subcontracting other companies to develop components on the spaceship, SpaceX saves a lot of cost by developing these components in-house resulting in much cheaper overall spaceships.
